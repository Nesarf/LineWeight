# The books, and how much of each could be read

**Why the distinction is recorded.** A book whose text extracts cleanly and a book that can only be learned by looking
at its pages are different kinds of evidence. **The first can be quoted; the second can only be looked at**, and a page
kept from it is the only way it can be consulted again. Marking the difference stops a page of pictures being cited as
though it were a paragraph.

**And the arrangement of the whole shelf, once read:**

- **Bradley** is the only one that gives folds as mechanics rather than as decoration.

# The articles, and which of them carry a rule

**Why a second shelf.** The books are somebody else's products, so only what was learned from them is kept. The articles
are the same kind of thing and are treated the same way: **what is stored is the URL and what each one gave**, not the
text. Ninety-two were read across ten categories and eight tags on two sites; **fifty of them carried a rule**, and the
rest were software walk-throughs whose subject is a menu rather than a drawing.

**A difference that matters when weighing them.** A book devotes a chapter to one subject; an article devotes a page. So
an article is good for **a single rule stated plainly** -- a proportion, a named line, a check -- and poor for a
procedure. Every entry below is one such rule.

## CLIP STUDIO PAINT (`clipstudio.net/painting`)

| category | articles | what it gave |
|---|---|---|
| `character-art` | 10 read | **the chibi rules**: two heads regardless of the character's set height, features below the midline, eyes enlarged to keep proportion, hands and feet short and fat, **the silhouette test**, one-stroke line, one layer per part |
| `digital-art` | 10 read | **the four elements of a pose** (strength and rhythm, the action line, construction, perspective) · **folds by fabric**: thick/thin, stiff/soft, heavy/light, smooth/rough, and folds pull toward seams · the definition of sketching as converting a complex shape into a simple one first |

> landed close to the same value.**

# The manufacturers, and what the paint itself does

**Why a third shelf.** The books teach drawing and the articles each carry one rule about making a picture. A paint
manufacturer's technical page is a different kind of evidence again: it is about **the material**, and the material does
not care what is being painted. Winsor & Newton say which of their blues covers and which one lets the ground through;
Gamblin say what a scumble is and why thin layers outlast thick ones. None of that changes between a still life and a
portrait, which is exactly what makes it worth having before the subject is chosen.

**Two limits, and they are different kinds.**

The first is that a manufacturer is selling something. Their diagrams demonstrate real behaviour -- the black line under
a pull-down is a fair test and its outcome can be seen in the photograph -- but a page that ends in a product link has
an interest in the reader's palette, and nothing here is recorded as a comparison between brands.

The second is more useful: **Phoenix Art Materials states its own limit in the article the other numbers came from.**
Their brand comparison is called an artist's observation rather than a controlled laboratory comparison and is not to be
used for product-wide ratings. That is worth more than the numbers themselves, because it is the sentence that says how
much of the rest can be believed.

| source | what it gave |
|---|---|
| **Winsor & Newton**, Masterclass, `oil-opaque-and-transparent` | **three same-family pairs**: cadmium/transparent yellow, cadmium red/permanent rose, cerulean/ultramarine · the test that settles it -- pull the colour over a black line, the opaque one erases it and the transparent one lets it show |
| **Winsor & Newton**, Masterclass, `the-art-of-glazing` | what a glaze is for: unifying light and dark and bringing luminosity out of the dark · its depth comes from how much pigment is in it and from layering, not from one heavier application · a transparent pigment is the only one that can do it |
| **Gamblin**, `Painting Glossary` | **tint / shade / tone as three operations with three different effects** · glaze, scumble, impasto, grisaille, imprimatura, underpainting, mass tone, value, chroma · **fat-over-lean**: under layers leaner than upper ones, so the film stays flexible and cracks less |
| **Gamblin**, `Building Permanent Paintings` | **multiple thin layers beat fewer thicker ones**, and the rule holds for ground, paint and varnish alike · thin with solvent *and* binder, because solvent alone leaves the layer too lean to adhere |
| **Phoenix Art Materials**, `oil-paint-opacity-undertone-tinting-strength` | **tinting strength is not pigment load** · it depends on pigment type, particle properties, dispersion, opacity and the white it is mixed into · a strong tint is efficient in a large mixture and hard to control in a delicate passage · evaluate with a tint ladder of at least three ratios |

**What these five add up to, said once.** Every painting script in this repository carried the same four-line
`mix(a, b, t)` -- a linear interpolation weighted by how much of each tube was used. The shelf says that weight is the
wrong quantity: strength is a property of the pigment and of the white it is mixed into, and a pigment can be opaque and
weak (cadmium yellow) or transparent and strong (ultramarine). That is one fact, from one page, and it is the only thing
on this shelf that any code here implements. The other four are checks a picture can be held against.

# Ink, and the question of where an edge comes from

**Why this is its own shelf and not a row in the articles table.** A user said, before this shelf existed, that a
sumi-e painting should be **a category of its own rather than a default style** -- and this is the page that gives that
category its vocabulary. It is also the only source here that comes from outside the European oil tradition, and the
difference is not a matter of taste: it is a different answer to the question every renderer in this repository has to
answer.

| source | what it gave |
|---|---|
| **CLIP STUDIO**, `articles/2850`, '单色艺术 - 水墨画 (墨絵) 技巧', by konijnsate | **the four styles** -- 工笔 gongbi, 写意 xieyi, 白描 baimiao, 没骨 mogu · **骨法**: line is the structure of a picture the way bone is the structure of a body, so a deformed line can ruin the whole thing · the working detail, which is that a texture layer goes on **first**, not last, because it sets the mood the painting is made in |

**The fourth style is the one this project has to think about.** Every edge in `lineweight`'s painting line comes from
one of two places: the natural end of a mark (the apple's round form, the fish body before the recount in 4.75) or a
hard clip (the cloth, 4.63). **没骨 mogu is a third: the edge is where the colour stops.** The article says it plainly --
colour laid down directly with no outline first, and "the lines may form unintentionally from the water's edge or
overlapping colours". A water medium does that by itself. A stamping renderer has to be told to, and telling it is a
different piece of work from this one.

**And 骨法 agrees with something reached here from the opposite direction.** `AESTHETIC-LIBRARY` 4.71 says a line
drawing is not a set of lines of assorted widths but a set of lines that *mean* different things. Xie He said it in
about 550 CE: the stroke is the bone, and a deformed bone deforms the body. Two sources for one principle is the whole
reason a facts file is worth keeping.

## The primary texts, in Chinese, public domain

**Why the originals and not a tutorial about them.** A modern article about Chinese painting paraphrases the canon; the
canon is short, public domain, and **already written as countable lists**. Every one of those lists names between two and
eleven things and gives each a clause of definition, and a paraphrase loses the clauses. Three texts carry almost all of
it:

| text | date | what it gave |
|---|---|---|
| **Xie He, 古畫品錄** | c. 550 | **六法**, in order, with the statement that holding all six is rare and that each painter is good at one of them |
| **Jing Hao, 筆法記** | c. 900 | **六要** with a gloss of under fifteen characters each · **筆四勢** with a one-clause test each and five ways to fail · **二病** split by whether the fault can be repaired · **the mountain vocabulary**: eleven terms, each defined by one word |
| **Guo Xi, 林泉高致** | c. 1080 | **三遠**, each with an eye position, a colour, a mass and a figure size · **遠山無皴，遠水無波，遠人無目** · **四可** ranked · four seasons of cloud and of mountain, each with one image |

**The thing these three do that no European oil manual on this shelf does: they define by subtraction.** 「遠山無皴」 is a
rule about what to leave out. 「似者，得其形，遺其氣」 separates a drawing that is correct from one that is true. A shelf
built only from the Western manuals would have taught this project how to put marks down and never how to leave them off.

**And the first of the 六法 is 氣韻生動 -- spirit resonance.** It is stated first and it is the only one of the six that no
checker here can measure. That is worth writing down beside the other five, because the purpose of this file is to know
what it cannot check.
